[Title] Pirate Sea Race Game Specification
[Subtitle] Game design and browser multiplayer implementation plan
Version 0.1 • 23 September 2026 • Proposed baseline for playtesting
Build an eight-player pirate treasure race in which players explore a shared sea, discover supernatural powers, collect four different poneglyph rubbings, and race to the final treasure. Rival pirates can interrupt that journey through short, skill-based duels and steal a missing rubbing from the loser.
The strongest part of the concept is the choice between exploration and confrontation. A player can seek a remote ruin, follow a rumor, chase someone carrying a missing clue, or take a risky route to the finish. Combat matters because of what is at stake in the world.
The recommended format combines a top-down overworld with side-view, single-plane fighting arenas. The match continues while two players duel. Defeat removes one objective item and some match currency, but preserves the player’s fruit, fighting kit, and remaining clues.
<TABLE>
Setting | Proposed first release
Players | 8 solo players per match
Session | Aim for 20–30 minutes; hard stop at 35 minutes
World | 12 explorable islands plus one hidden final island
Objective | Hold one rubbing of each of four distinct types
Victory | Reach the treasure and complete a 10-second claim
Battles | Instanced 1v1 duels; one 60-second round
Platforms | Desktop browsers; keyboard first; controller support
Progression | Match power resets; account rewards are cosmetic
</TABLE>
All numerical values in this document are initial tuning parameters, not validated balance or performance results. One Piece terms describe the requested fantasy. The proposed production direction uses original characters, island names, power names, art, music, and treasure lore. Brand and licensing choices remain a separate product decision.
This specification defines the playable rules, system boundaries, edge cases, implementation structure, and release gates.
[Heading1] 1 The player experience
[Heading2] Core loop
Spawn at a fair starting location, sail toward a lead, explore an island, gain a power or rubbing, assess nearby rivals, fight or evade, and choose the next destination. Once all four types are held, the compass reveals the final treasure. The player must still travel there and defend the claim.
Three decisions should repeatedly matter: where to go next, whether a fight is worth the lost time, and which abilities suit the player’s approach. Sailing should create encounters and anticipation; it must not become several minutes of empty travel.
[Heading2] A representative match
A player starts beside a small ship, finds a Smoke fruit on a nearby island, and reads a rumor pointing to a western ruin. Another player reaches that ruin first and leaves with the Moon rubbing. The first player can chase them or investigate a second lead.
Later, the Smoke player wins a boarding duel and steals the Moon rubbing. The defeated opponent keeps their other two rubbings and Ember powers. They respawn away from the winner and pursue a different Moon source. A third player completes all four types, triggering a public rumor about the final island’s sector. The remaining players must decide whether to finish their own collection or intercept the leader.
[Heading2] Design commitments
[ListBullet] Exploration and fighting are both viable ways to advance. A player can win without initiating PvP, but cannot assume they will avoid it.
[ListBullet] Fruit powers change play style. Finding the rarest-looking power must not automatically decide the match.
[ListBullet] PvP produces a meaningful setback without erasing the loser’s whole match.
[ListBullet] The destination is earned through information and inventory. Defeating the most opponents is not a substitute victory condition.
[ListBullet] Every important attack has readable startup and a defensive answer available to every kit.
[ListBullet] The first prototype must prove the online duel and the theft loop before adding a large content roster.
[Heading2] Scope boundary
The first release is a repeatable session game. Persistent shared worlds, crews, ranked seasons, elaborate naval combat, trading, sprawling quests, and mobile touch combat are later possibilities. Each would materially change balancing or production requirements.
[Heading1] 2 Match rules and pacing
<TABLE>
Phase | Rule and intended effect
Lobby | Public queue or private invitation code; region and connection quality shown
Ready check | 20 seconds after all eight seats are filled; clients preload arenas
Departure | 45 seconds of protection; players can explore and collect starter loot
Exploration | All 12 initial rubbing sources are available; no time gate on collection
First full set | Final-island sector announced; eligible player receives exact navigation
Minute 20 | Reveal sectors containing unclaimed rubbings and abandoned rubbing caches
Minute 28 | Publish approximate sectors of rubbing carriers every 30 seconds
Minute 35 | Stop the match; if nobody claimed the treasure, record no winner
</TABLE>
The match timer begins when player control starts. Eight-player public matches do not start short-handed in version one. Private tests may start with two to eight players and are marked as custom matches. Late arrivals wait in the lobby for another match; they cannot enter an ongoing race.
[Heading2] Spawn fairness
Select one of eight prevalidated outer-world spawn slots for each player. Randomize player assignment, island variants, and loot sockets within constraints. Give every player a ship, the same base fighting kit, 50 gold, and a rumor leading toward a reachable objective. Spawn protection permits starter loot and sailing, but collecting a rubbing or initiating a duel ends it immediately.
Every slot must offer an initial useful pickup within 20–40 seconds and a plausible rubbing route within 90–150 seconds. Adjacent starting ships must not be in engagement range. These are travel-time targets to validate in playtests, not fixed guarantees based only on map distance.
[Heading2] Time limits and results
There is no automatic win for having the most clues at the deadline. A timeout is a draw for victory purposes; the result screen still shows exploration and duel accomplishments. If draws are common, revise travel times and information availability before adding a different scoring system.
If a claim completes on the exact deadline tick, process an already-valid completed claim before the timeout. A duel still running at the deadline ends without theft. No match timer is paused for duels, loading, or individual disconnections.
[Heading1] 3 The sea and islands
Use a top-down tile world with a fixed navigable layout and authored island templates. Randomize their contents and approved placements. Avoid unrestricted procedural geography in the first release: travel fairness and recognizable landmarks are more valuable than unlimited map variation.
<TABLE>
Element | Initial content and behavior
Sea | Connected routes, shoals as obstacles, clear docking areas
Islands | 12 islands drawn from tropical, volcanic, ruin, and mist themes
Final island | One of several validated hidden sockets; reachable by every ship
Ruins | One globally claimable rubbing source on each regular island
Other loot | Fruit pedestals, supply chests, rumor signs, and gold caches
Landmarks | Visible silhouettes that help players navigate without opening a map
Hazards | Clearly marked slow zones; no lethal environmental traps in version one
</TABLE>
[Heading2] Movement and visibility
Sailing uses acceleration, braking, and turning, with generous handling rather than a naval simulation. Nearby ships and islands are visible inside a defined sight radius. Unvisited territory is covered by fog on the minimap. Once discovered, terrain stays on the personal map, while enemies disappear outside current visibility.
World positions are authoritative on the server. Island discovery, enemy visibility, and rumors are evaluated there. The client receives only the hidden-state information it is entitled to know. Public terrain can be shared; objective locations and the final-island choice must not be recoverable from a public random seed.
[Heading2] Exploration activities
Each ruin uses one of two short interactions: a three-symbol sequence or a small navigation puzzle. Reading nearby clues solves it; no external One Piece knowledge is required. Completing it permits a six-second rubbing interaction that can be interrupted by a valid encounter. Total time at a fresh ruin should be about 20–40 seconds.
Rumors identify an island or sector containing a type the player lacks. Stale rumors are visibly marked after their target is claimed. The journal offers a replacement lead from remaining sources or a coarse carrier sighting. It never guarantees that the destination will remain uncontested.
Target neighboring-island travel at 20–40 seconds and the longest useful crossing at 90 seconds or less. Tune map size around those times. Remove empty stretches before adding more sailing mechanics.
[Heading1] 4 Poneglyph ownership and theft
Poneglyphs are stationary ancient inscriptions. Players carry their physical rubbings. Use four distinct symbols: Sun, Moon, Tide, and Storm. The match contains three individually identified rubbings of each type, for 12 total. Each of the 12 regular islands begins with one sealed source; assignment is server-controlled.
<TABLE>
Rule | Exact behavior
Collection | Hold interact for six uninterrupted seconds at an unlocked source
Inventory | At most one rubbing of each type per player
Duplicates | Cannot collect or steal a type already held; it stays with its owner
Completion | Holding all four types enables final navigation and claiming
Normal defeat | Winner chooses one loser-held type that the winner lacks
Theft deadline | Five-second choice; default to the eligible type with fewest free sources, then fixed type order
No eligible type | No rubbing transfers; the gold reward still applies
Draw | Neither player loses rubbings or gold
Dropping or trading | Players cannot voluntarily drop, trade, copy, or destroy rubbings
</TABLE>
Each rubbing has exactly one location: sealed at a source, owned by a player, held in a recovery cache, or reserved inside an unresolved duel transaction. Its unique ID persists through every transfer. There is no new rubbing spawn when a player loses.
[Heading2] Why multiple copies and limited theft
A single global copy of each type would let one player or an abandoned session block the whole match. Three copies allow competing routes to victory. Limiting theft to one type preserves tension without turning a single mistake into a full reset. Players cannot hoard duplicates to remove every route for everyone else.
This is a proposed tradeoff: the match has less total-loot brutality than an elimination game, but more opportunities for recovery and rivalry. Measure whether exploration alone is too fast before reducing the supply.
[Heading2] Transfer invariants
Commit a duel result and its inventory changes together, once, on the authoritative match process. A repeated result message must not repeat a transfer. Validate winner eligibility again at commit time. The 12 rubbing IDs must remain accounted for until the match ends, including when a player quits during resolution.
[Heading1] 5 Finding and claiming the final treasure
When a player first holds all four types, their compass reveals the final island’s exact route. The match announces that a complete set exists and reveals the island’s broad sea sector to everyone. All ships may travel there; four rubbings are required to claim the treasure, not to physically enter the island.
This creates a final interception opportunity without requiring every challenger to have completed their own collection. A challenger with fewer than four can stop a leader through a duel, but cannot win until they complete their own set.
[Heading2] Claim sequence
[ListNumber] Reach the treasure circle while holding all four rubbing types.
[ListNumber] End any protection state, stop moving, and hold interact for ten seconds.
[ListNumber] Remain connected, eligible, and free of an accepted duel reservation for the entire channel.
[ListNumber] On completion, the server commits the winner and freezes all match outcomes.
Only one claim channel can be active at a time. A second eligible player must interrupt through a valid challenge or wait. Characters do not body-block one another in the overworld. Moving, losing eligibility, disconnecting, or entering a duel resets claim progress to zero.
[Heading2] Counterplay and information
Anyone in engagement range can interrupt a claim by successfully starting a duel. The claim UI shows this risk explicitly. Duels remain 1v1: surrounding players cannot damage the fighters or join halfway through. After a duel, the winner receives eight seconds to move away; objective interactions end that protection.
Losing a rubbing removes claim eligibility immediately. The game cannot make a person forget a destination they have already learned; the server simply refuses an ineligible claim. Final navigation becomes inactive until the set is restored.
[Heading2] Resolving simultaneous actions
On each world tick, process accepted encounter reservations before advancing claim channels. If several attackers qualify in the same tick, choose the closest, then the lowest stable player ID. If several players begin claiming on the same tick, use the same distance-and-ID rule. Inputs arriving after a resolved win cannot retroactively cancel it.
The ten-second channel is a deliberate addition to the original “arrive and win” idea. Test both versions. Keep the channel only if it creates exciting finishes without repeated, frustrating stalls.
[Heading1] 6 Starting and leaving encounters
Exploration and combat use different views, but remain parts of one live match. Each player can belong to at most one duel. Their overworld avatar becomes a non-interactive encounter marker while the duel runs, and their ship is reserved. Other players continue exploring.
[Heading2] On islands
A player presses the challenge action within close range and line of sight. A visible 0.6-second lunge telegraphs the attempt. The defender can move or dash out of its hit area. If it connects, the server reserves both players and starts the duel. Missing incurs a six-second challenge cooldown.
[Heading2] At sea
Ships use a short-range grappling action with a one-second windup and a visible aim direction. A successful grapple reserves both ships and loads the shared ship-deck arena. Escape is possible before the grapple lands. There are no cannons, sinking ships, crew stations, or separate ship health in version one.
[Heading2] Reservation and transition
Eligibility requires both players to be active, close enough, unprotected, and outside another duel. A reservation atomically locks their inventories. Use a three-second transition with a visible countdown; all battle assets should already be loaded. If one client fails to become ready within ten seconds, resolve that client as forfeiting. If both fail because the server is unhealthy, cancel the encounter without rewards.
The dueling players enter with full duel health, fresh stamina, and the same starting Haki meter. World pursuit positioning does not grant a free opening hit. World boosts are suspended in duels and cannot expire into different combat statistics midway through a fight.
[Heading2] Return to the world
The winner returns to the encounter location with eight seconds of escape protection. The loser respawns after 15 seconds at a validated dock away from nearby opponents, with 20 seconds of protection. A draw returns both to separate nearby points with eight seconds of protection. Objective collection, claiming, or initiating an attack ends return protection first and is then processed normally.
Keep a visible duel marker and elapsed time in the world. Live spectating is deferred to reduce stream-sniping information and avoid extra networking scope in the first build.
[Heading1] 7 Fighting system
The target is the satisfaction of spacing, blocking, punishing, and reading an opponent, using a single-plane 2D arena. Start with compact move lists and short combinations. A large move catalogue and complex aerial juggling would multiply animation and balance work before the core is proven.
<TABLE>
System | Starting rule
Round | One round; 60-second clock; 1,000 health each
Timeout | Higher remaining health wins
Equal health | Ten-second overtime; then compare health again; exact tie is a draw
Movement | Walk, crouch, jump, forward dash, backdash; auto-face opponent
Attacks | Light, heavy, low, launcher, anti-air, throw, two kit specials
Defense | Hold back to guard; down-back guards low; universal throw escape
Stamina | 100 maximum; powers movement and guard integrity
Haki meter | 100 maximum; starts at 25; powers armor, parry, and finisher
Combos | Initial cap of five damaging hits before forced recovery
</TABLE>
Standing guard blocks high and mid attacks. Crouching guard blocks lows, avoids highs, and loses to mids. Throws beat passive guard and have a ten-frame escape window. A universal anti-air discourages repeated jumping. Guarding costs stamina; a broken guard causes a short punishable stagger, never a full-health guaranteed combo.
[Heading2] Damage and recovery
Use 45 damage for a basic jab and roughly 100–150 for a committed heavy or special before scaling. Apply successive combo scaling of 100, 80, 65, 50, and 40 percent. Target ordinary successful combinations at 15–25 percent of health. The fifth damaging hit forces a non-juggleable recovery state; implement the cap in the simulation, not only in animations.
Dashes cost 20 stamina; blocking a light costs 10, a heavy 25, and a special 20. Regenerate 20 per second after one second without spending or blocking. Guard break causes 30 frames of stagger and restores 30 stamina. No healing works inside a duel. Every kit must answer throws, armor, projectiles, and evasion.
Both simultaneous lethal hits produce a draw. A KO on the final round tick takes priority over timeout comparison. The server evaluates both players’ hit events for the tick before declaring a KO, so processing order cannot invent a winner.
[Heading1] 8 Controls and readable combat timing
<TABLE>
Action | Keyboard default | Controller default
Move | WASD or arrow keys | D-pad or left stick
Interact and dock | E | South face button in world
Challenge or grapple | J | West face button in world
Light attack | J | West face button
Heavy attack | K | North face button
Special one | U | South face button in duel
Special two | I | East face button
Dash | Shift | Right bumper
Haki modifier | Space | Left bumper
Throw | J and K | Light and heavy together
Journal and map | Tab | View or select button
</TABLE>
Use down plus heavy for low sweep, forward plus heavy for launcher, and down plus light for anti-air. Allow full remapping and display action names in the tutorial. Check keyboard rollover combinations. Both horizontal directions together produce neutral, independent of message order.
[Heading2] Initial frame budgets
Combat logic advances at 60 fixed steps per second regardless of render rate. A frame is approximately 16.7 milliseconds. Start with these shared baseline attacks; fruit-specific exceptions must be written into their data definitions.
<TABLE>
Move | Startup | Active | Recovery | On block
Jab | 7 frames | 3 frames | 12 frames | Minus 2
Heavy mid | 18 frames | 4 frames | 24 frames | Minus 12
Low sweep | 20 frames | 3 frames | 26 frames | Minus 15
Projectile special | 22 frames | Projectile life | 30 frames | Spacing dependent
Throw | 12 frames | 2 frames | 24 frames | Not guardable
</TABLE>
“Minus 12” means the attacker recovers 12 frames later than the defender, creating a punish opportunity. Hitstun, blockstun, cancel windows, hurtboxes, and hitboxes live in move data and must agree with these budgets.
Use a four-frame input buffer and no mandatory motion-input sequences in the first release. Show clear startup silhouettes, distinguish low attacks from mids, and provide separate feedback for guard, parry, armor, and confirmed damage. Cosmetic effects must not hide the actionable part of an attack.
[Heading1] 9 Devil fruit inspired power kits
Players begin fruitless with a complete Duelist kit. Eating a fruit replaces the two kit specials. Core normals, throw, guard, and universal Haki remain. All six fruit types below are first-release candidates; the first playable slice uses Ember and Smoke only.
<TABLE>
Kit | Two signature specials | Advantage | Vulnerability
Duelist | Advancing thrust and counter slash | Reliable close combat and meter use | Limited long-range pressure
Ember | Flame bolt and rising flare | Controls space and punishes jumps | Long recovery if attacks miss
Smoke | Short feint dash and smoke screen | Changes spacing and obscures intent | Low direct damage; readable reappearance
Spring | Charged lunge and rebound kick | Fast approach with varied timing | Charge can be interrupted
Stone | Armored shoulder and ground burst | Wins selected close-range trades | Slow recovery; vulnerable to throws
Gale | Gust projectile and retreating slash | Pushes opponents and resets distance | Weak when trapped close
Beast | Pounce and claw sequence | Sustained close-range pressure | Telegraphs approach; limited reach
</TABLE>
[Heading2] Acquisition and commitment
Place 12 fruit pedestals, two of each type, across fair loot routes. They are distinct from rubbing sources. Eating takes three seconds and can be interrupted. A player may inspect the kit description before committing. After a fruit is eaten, it stays for that match; there is no fruit swapping or random reroll economy in version one.
Fruit users remain viable after defeat because their fruit is retained. Fruitless Duelist remains a deliberate fighting option. Its specials must be tested against every fruit; it is not a weak starting class to escape as soon as possible.
[Heading2] Constraints on powers
No permanent intangibility, unlimited flight, unavoidable screen-wide damage, automatic stun loops, or uncounterable invisibility. Smoke effects cannot hide the opponent’s health bar or remove all visual tells. Armor absorbs one eligible hit rather than making the whole move invulnerable; throws and specified armor-break attacks still work.
For the first release, fruit abilities are combat-only. This avoids a mobility fruit also becoming the best exploration and escape choice. Ships and docks handle travel; swimming and drowning are outside the initial rules. Later exploration abilities should open optional shortcuts while leaving the four objective types reachable by every kit.
[Heading1] 10 Haki and match progression
Haki is the common tactical layer that lets every player answer powerful fruit abilities. It is available from the beginning; finding a rare fruit must not be the only path to competent combat.
<TABLE>
Ability | Initial cost | Effect and counterplay
Armament | 30 Haki | Empower the next heavy within three seconds; breaks one-hit armor; blockable
Observation | 40 Haki | Six-frame parry window against strikes; loses to throws; 24-frame recovery on miss
Resolve finisher | 100 Haki | Large telegraphed attack; can be guarded or evaded; scales in combos
</TABLE>
Suggested inputs are Haki plus heavy, Haki plus dash, and Haki plus both specials. A missed parry still spends meter. There is no passive aura that negates all fruit skills, no hidden random critical hit, and no always-on damage increase from account level.
[Heading2] Meter economy
Each duel starts at 25 Haki. A confirmed hit grants five meter to its attacker and three to its defender; a successful parry grants ten. Whiffing and standing still grant none. A damage instance awards meter once, including repeated projectile contacts. Cap gain at 20 per combo for either player. Unspent meter does not carry between duels.
[Heading2] Limited match progression
Players can find training scrolls through island exploration or buy them with match gold. One scroll selects a Haki focus: Armament costs five less, Observation costs five less, or successful guards generate a small extra amount of meter. Only one focus can be equipped; changing it requires a dock interaction. No focus changes the player’s maximum health or base damage.
Training-scroll availability is tied to exploration and limited shop stock per player, not repeated kills. The first slice omits focuses entirely until base combat is balanced. The full eight-player alpha introduces the three choices if they create meaningful preferences.
[Heading2] Account progression
Persistent rewards unlock visual customization, titles, emotes, and journal milestones. They do not grant starting rubbings, extra health, stronger fruits, better ships, or exclusive competitive moves. Saved controls, accessibility settings, and practice progress carry between sessions. Match gold, items, and powers reset every match.
[Heading1] 11 Economy and comeback rules
Keep the match economy small. Its job is to create route choices and help players recover information, without overwhelming the central rubbing race.
<TABLE>
Source or purchase | Starting value | Rule
Starting purse | 50 gold | Same for every player
Supply cache | 25 gold | One global claim per cache
Fresh ruin completion | 40 gold | Once per player per ruin; cannot farm the interaction
PvP victory | 20 percent of loser gold, capped at 50 | Transfer existing gold; never mint a kill reward
Targeted rumor | 25 gold | Lead to a missing rubbing type
Haki focus scroll | 75 gold | Full alpha only; one focus equipped
Sail tonic | 30 gold | Plus 10 percent sailing speed for 12 seconds
</TABLE>
Gold transfer rounds down and cannot make a balance negative. Shops are dockside objects, not permanent safe zones. Buying is a brief interaction that can be interrupted. Purchases have an idempotent request ID so retries cannot double-spend.
[Heading2] Consumables
Carry at most two tonics; only one effect may be active. Tonics do not stack, work only at sea, and cannot be activated while being grappled or in a duel. A tonic does not provide damage, invulnerability, or faster treasure claiming. Losing a duel does not drop the remaining consumables.
[Heading2] Preventing runaway leaders
The winner steals only one missing type, cannot hoard duplicates, and gains no permanent combat level from kills. Fruit powers and maximum duel health survive defeat. Respawn protection and remote spawn selection prevent immediate repeated attacks. Fighting also costs world time while rivals keep exploring.
When a player loses a rubbing, give them a free fresh rumor for that missing type. If all other copies are carried, show a coarse, delayed carrier sighting rather than an exact continuous tracker. This helps them choose a recovery route without handing them a free replacement.
Suppress gold rewards for repeated victories over the same opponent within three minutes. Rubbing theft still follows normal eligibility. Track suspicious repeated forfeits for review; do not automatically punish players merely because friends often encounter one another in private games.
[Heading1] 12 Defeat disconnection and failure
<TABLE>
Event | Required behavior
Normal duel loss | Transfer one eligible rubbing and gold; retain kit; respawn after 15 seconds
Disconnect in world | Cancel channels; avatar remains vulnerable; reserve seat for 60 seconds
Disconnect in duel | Neutral inputs after held-input timeout; fight continues; forfeit after 10 seconds absent
Return within reservation | Reattach same player identity to the current authoritative state
Reservation expires | Drop remaining rubbings into a visible recovery cache; remove player
Explicit quit | Immediate duel forfeit if needed; then release remaining rubbings into a cache
Server process failure | Abort affected match in first release; no winner or competitive penalty
Both duel clients absent | Cancel unresolved rewards if both forfeit on the same tick; release caches on expiry
</TABLE>
[Heading2] Details that prevent exploits
After 250 milliseconds without a valid input stream, clear held movement and attack buttons. Disconnecting cannot keep a character attacking indefinitely, pause the match, or protect their inventory. A disconnected world avatar can be challenged; if they remain absent, the ordinary forfeit rule applies.
If a disconnected player loses a duel, process the normal one-rubbing transfer first, move the player to their respawn state, and preserve the original seat-reservation deadline. At expiry, place their remaining rubbings in a recovery cache at that respawn dock. Do not duplicate the already-transferred rubbing.
A cache is placed on the nearest reachable shoreline or dock if its intended position is invalid. It never despawns during the match. Each rubbing can be collected only by a player missing that type. A six-second interaction claims one selected rubbing at a time. An empty cache disappears.
[Heading2] Server health versus individual connection quality
Individual packet loss is not a reason to cancel a losing fight. A server-detected simulation stall affecting the encounter is a separate fault. Cancel that duel without transfers, restore both pre-duel inventories, and return participants safely. Log the fault and match revision.
The first release stores final outcomes, not resumable live-world snapshots. A process restart cannot be described as a reconnect recovery. Add crash recovery only after the basic match and transaction model are proven. If everyone leaves, close the room and mark it abandoned with no winner.

[Heading2] Onboarding and accessibility
Offer a five-minute solo introduction: move, sail, dock, complete a ruin, guard a heavy, punish it, and understand theft. Let experienced players skip it. Provide a practice arena with an idle or scripted defensive opponent, hitbox visualization for developers, and a reset button.
Use shapes and names as well as colors for rubbing types. Allow larger UI, reduced screen shake, reduced flashing, separate audio sliders, and full control remapping. Supply visual equivalents for grapple warnings and claim alarms. Ensure menus work with keyboard navigation and use readable DOM text where practical.
[Heading2] Visual direction and production budget
Use a coherent original 2D anime-pirate style with strong silhouettes, bright seas, and readable island themes. Start with one shared humanoid rig or sprite base and cosmetic variations. Ship-deck and island duels share one collision layout with two backgrounds. Save unusual body proportions and bespoke movesets for later.
Budget roughly 18–24 shared character animation actions, plus fruit-specific effects and any required unique poses. Track walk, crouch, jump, dash, guard, hit reactions, knockdown, throw, normals, and specials in an asset manifest. A generated concept image is not a finished gameplay sprite set; animation consistency and hitbox alignment need production work.
Sound should communicate interaction timing: distinct cues for launch, impact, guard, parry, grapple warning, rubbing theft, and a full set. Unlock browser audio after the player’s first input. The initial client should preload shared combat assets and stream optional island decoration later. Art cannot delay the first control response by requiring the whole world to load.
[Heading1] 14 Browser architecture
Use TypeScript across the client, shared simulation, and server. Phaser supplies the 2D presentation layer. Colyseus provides authoritative rooms, matchmaking, and state synchronization. Its current documentation also describes prediction and reconciliation primitives; test the selected version against the duel requirements before committing to its APIs. [1–3]
<TABLE>
Component | Responsibility
Browser client | Render world and arenas, collect inputs, predict local motion, display UI
Web shell | Lobby, account settings, journal UI, loading, accessibility menus
Match process | Own the world, visibility, inventory, timers, encounter state, and winner
Combat simulation | Pure fixed-step functions shared between client and server
HTTP service | Sessions, match allocation, profiles, and final results
PostgreSQL | Accounts, cosmetics, match summaries, and moderation records
Static hosting | Versioned client bundle, sprites, audio, and maps
Monitoring | Room counts, tick timings, errors, transfer audit, and latency metrics
</TABLE>
[Heading2] Match and duel boundaries
Start with one authoritative Colyseus room per match. Duels are isolated simulation objects within that room, not separate servers or independent inventory owners. A match can contain at most four simultaneous 1v1 duels. Fighters receive their arena stream and permitted match announcements; other players receive only an encounter marker.
This removes cross-service inventory handoffs from the first implementation. The room creates a duel, freezes the participants’ objective inventories, validates the outcome, commits theft, and returns them to the world. It is the only writer for live match state.
[Heading2] Deployment baseline
For an initial private test, start with a single regional host around four vCPUs and 8 GB RAM, serving the game process behind HTTPS and secure WebSockets. Treat this as a test configuration, not a concurrent-user capacity promise. Keep static assets on a suitable static host and avoid database queries inside simulation ticks.
As usage grows, distribute whole matches across workers and regions. Keep each live room on its assigned process. Add shared presence or matchmaking infrastructure only when multiple workers require it. Drain existing matches during deployment instead of terminating them. Check the selected hosting platform supports long-lived WebSockets and persistent game processes.
[Heading1] 15 Networking for exploration and duels
Exploration tolerates interpolation better than a close-range fighter. Use separate update policies even though both modes share one match authority. WebSockets are a practical first browser transport, but reliable ordered delivery can stall behind lost packets; test that failure mode before promising fighting-game responsiveness. [4]
<TABLE>
Layer | Initial engineering target
Render | 60 frames per second on the declared reference laptop
World simulation | 20 authoritative steps per second
World updates | Up to 10–20 updates per second, filtered by visibility
Duel simulation | 60 fixed steps per second
Duel input stream | One frame-tagged input per step, batched when needed
Duel corrections | Authoritative checkpoints plus accepted-input acknowledgements
Client history | Two seconds of serializable duel states and inputs
Region selection | Prefer stable round-trip latency below 100 milliseconds
</TABLE>
[Heading2] Authoritative prediction policy
The server alone decides hits, health, inventories, and winners. Both duel clients run the same deterministic step function to predict their local experience. On a checkpoint mismatch, restore the authoritative state and replay unacknowledged inputs. Snapshot the complete coupled duel state, including projectiles, hitstop, timers, and random-generator state.
Select a shared input buffer before each duel from three to eight frames, based on the slower connection’s measured delay and jitter. Tune this policy in the spike. Late input cannot rewrite a completed server frame or committed outcome. Repeat held state for at most 250 milliseconds, then substitute neutral. Validate sequences and reject future-frame spam.
This is server-authoritative prediction and reconciliation. It is not a promise of peer-to-peer GGPO behavior. GGPO’s design illustrates why deterministic stepping, state restoration, and replay are important; choosing a different authority model still requires its own latency validation. [5]
Colyseus documents built-in prediction and rewind facilities, but the game still owns its deterministic logic and fairness policy. Do not assume generic movement reconciliation automatically produces good melee trades, throw escapes, or simultaneous hits. Prototype those interactions first. [2–3]
Use one 60 Hz match clock, with world logic every third step. Separate simulation from Phaser physics, render timing, particles, audio, and wall-clock calls. Replayed events use stable IDs so corrections do not play the same sound or award a pickup twice.

[Heading2] Client intent and server decisions
Clients send actions such as move, interact with a target, attempt a grapple, submit combat buttons, choose a theft type, or start a claim. They never send authoritative damage amounts, owned rubbing lists, gold balances, final positions, or a declared victory. The server derives every consequence from validated intent.
Theft selection refers to a server-created duel result and an eligible type; it is not a general transfer endpoint. Duplicate, stale, unauthorized, or already-resolved requests return the current result without mutating state. Inventory reservations prevent the same rubbing from being collected, stolen, or abandoned twice during a transition.
[Heading2] Hidden information
Keep enemy inventories, undiscovered loot, exact carrier positions, and final-island selection out of unauthorized state payloads. A minimap overlay is not a security boundary. Use explicit per-player views and test actual network messages. Colyseus state views are a candidate mechanism for this filtering. [6]
[Heading2] Operational controls
Validate message schemas, cap message size and frequency, enforce session ownership, and use expiring reconnect credentials. A public guest can have only one active match seat per session. Guests may play private tests; stronger account controls can precede public competitive play.
Record input logs and authoritative event traces around disputes and errors, with a short documented retention period. Restrict administrative access and avoid logging authentication tokens. Treat match time and inventory as server data even if the browser’s JavaScript is modified. Minification is not an anti-cheat strategy.

Organize the project around one shared rules implementation. Keep UI and rendering dependencies out of authoritative logic so the same match can be simulated headlessly in tests.

[Heading2] Content definitions
Define each move with an ID, input condition, startup frames, active frames, recovery, hitbox timeline, damage, hitstun, blockstun, knockback, cancel rules, stamina cost, Haki effect, and audiovisual references. Validate references and impossible timings at build time.
Island templates declare spawn sockets, walkable areas, docks, interaction positions, and navigation constraints. A generation validator checks reachability and rubbing supply before a match is admitted. Author maps in a tile editor and export a documented format; avoid hardcoding every island into client scenes.
[Heading2] Versioning and release workflow
Assign a rules version and content hash to every match. The client must match the server’s protocol and required content before joining. Pin package versions after the technical spike rather than building on unbounded latest dependencies. Keep prior static client assets available while old matches drain.
Use automated checks for simulation invariants, schema validation, deterministic replays, and production builds. Follow with manual cross-browser playtests of animation readability, input handling, and sound. Balance data changes require repeatable combat scenarios and match playtests, not only passing unit tests.

The slice deliberately uses two types and eight rubbings, with two sources per island, to test the entire loop quickly. The final-alpha configuration restores four types and 12 sources. Keep this difference in a rules preset so slice shortcuts cannot silently ship into the main mode.
[Heading2] First playable content
One character base, one combat collision layout, two arena backgrounds, four small islands, one ship, two fruit kits, a usable Duelist kit, basic ruins, and minimal sound. Use placeholder art if necessary, but attacks still need readable anticipation and impact.
[Heading2] Features after the core is proven
Potential additions include two-player crews, bosses guarding alternative clues, stronger sea events, additional fruits, fruit awakenings, advanced practice tools, spectator support, ranked matchmaking, and mobile controls. A crew mode needs its own shared-inventory and win rules; it is not a simple lobby-size option.
[Heading2] People and planning assumptions
The critical skills are game programming, real-time networking, 2D animation, UI, sound, and playtesting. For planning, allow roughly 2–4 engineer-weeks for the combat spike, 3–5 for the complete slice, and another 6–12 for an initial alpha, with art and testing running alongside. These are rough effort ranges, not delivery commitments or validated estimates.
Re-estimate after the combat spike. A polished fighting game can demand much more animation and balance work than its simple 2D appearance suggests. Expanding the fruit roster before the spike passes is the most avoidable scope risk.

<TABLE>
Area | Acceptance criterion

Completion | Full collection, theft, recovery, and claim work across multiple simultaneous duels
Endgame | Claim interrupts, lost eligibility, same-tick outcomes, and deadline ordering are deterministic
Disconnects | Rejoin, forfeit, cache release, and quit cannot erase or duplicate rubbings

[Heading2] Network and performance targets
Exercise 30, 80, and 150 millisecond round-trip latency; 0, 20, and 50 millisecond jitter; and 0, 1, and 3 percent packet loss. Report melee trades, throw escapes, correction frequency, and player judgments of fairness. These are test scenarios, not a claim that every combination will be acceptable.
Before public beta, target a p95 duel simulation step below eight milliseconds under the declared supported room load, with tick-overrun alarms. Target 60 FPS on an explicitly recorded reference laptop. Initially budget 20 MB or less of compressed assets before first play and prefetch the remaining required match content. Measure download-to-control time on a declared test connection.
[Heading2] Gameplay telemetry
Across at least 30 complete eight-player playtest matches, measure match duration, time to first useful pickup, first rubbing, first duel, average sailing gaps, abandonment after defeat, and the share of rubbings obtained through theft. Track which spawn slots and fruit kits overperform; small samples identify questions, not final proof of balance.
Aim for at least 80 percent of matches to produce a treasure winner before the deadline and at least 60 percent of defeated players to collect or steal another rubbing afterward. Use these as initial design hypotheses. If too few fights happen, improve encounter opportunities; if too many happen, protect time for exploration.
The core decision is whether players enjoy choosing between another island and another fight. If they describe sailing as waiting or combat as an arbitrary interruption, revise the transition and pacing before investing in more islands or powers.
